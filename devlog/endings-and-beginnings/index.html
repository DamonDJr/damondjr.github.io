Word endings make terrible word beginnings

Word Wars has one rule, and it fits in a sentence: the last letters of the word you type are stamped onto a block that lands on your opponent’s board, and they can only clear it with a word that starts with those letters.

The sentence took a minute to write. Making it true took most of the project.

English does not cooperate

Endings are a small set. Beginnings are a large one. The rule asks the small set to behave like the large one, and it will not.

Type RUNNING and look at what its tail actually offers: NING, ING, NG, G. Exactly one of those is a beginning anybody can use. The rest are not rare, they are impossible — no useful number of English words start with NG.

So RUNNING has a pool of one. However clever the selection on top of it, it brands ING. Every time.

The wall of ING and LY

The first version took the longest answerable tail on every attack, which sounds correct and is not. The longest tails are the most generic ones. Boards filled up with ING and LY and TION and ING again, and the game quietly stopped being a word game — you were not reading the board any more, you were typing the same four answers on a loop.

A rule that hands you the same problem every time is not a rule. It is a chore.

What “answerable” had to mean

A stamp is a promise that somebody can answer it, so the promise got a number. A fragment is only allowed onto a block if at least 40 words in the full dictionary begin with it, and at least 6 in the common list do.

The dictionary floor keeps it legal. The common-list floor keeps it findable — there is no point in a stamp that can only be cleared by somebody who happens to know an obscure word. Single letters are held higher again, at 30 common words rather than 6, because a one-letter stamp is nearly free to answer and should stay a rarity rather than a get-out.

Looking just off the end

Raising the standard does not solve the pool-of-one problem. RUNNING still only has ING.

So the search stopped being strictly about suffixes. It now also considers a few windows from near the end — a letter or two in from the tail.

That is what rescues SHIPMENTS, whose true tail is a bare S and nothing else. Nudge the window in by one letter and MENT appears, which is a real beginning with real answers. It is the same trick that makes FIX playable, its only true tail being an X that almost nothing starts with.

One guard: a window may never start at the front of the word. MUSIC must not brand a block MUS, because MUS is just MUSIC with the end chopped off, and being handed back the word you were about to type is not a puzzle. Short words are exempt, since a three-letter word has no meaningful middle.

Shifted windows are also a harsher thing to be given than an honest suffix, so they have to clear double the common-word floor before they qualify.

Choosing between whatever survives

What is left goes into a weighted draw rather than a sort:

  • Longer stamps weigh more — length squared — so they stay favoured without winning automatically.
  • A shifted window is worth 0.6 of a true tail, because it is the less honest kind.
  • A one-letter stamp is halved again on top of that.
  • Anything the defender is already dealing with, or that either side threw recently, drops to 0.15.

That last line is what actually broke the wall. ING did not become rare. It stopped being the answer twice in a row, which turns out to be the part that mattered.

What it changed

The board reads differently now. You are not answering the same four endings all match, so you have to look at what actually landed — and the moment you have to look, the rule starts doing the job it was written for. Your endings become their beginnings, and neither of you gets to stop thinking about it.
